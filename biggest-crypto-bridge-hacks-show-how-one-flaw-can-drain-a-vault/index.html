Biggest crypto bridge hacks show how one flaw can drain a vault

The biggest crypto bridge hacks came from stolen keys and flawed message checks; one approval failure could drain a vault and leave wrapped tokens unbacked.

The Crypto Front Page DeskNov 19, 2026 · 05:49 UTC4 min read

The biggest crypto bridge hacks usually began with stolen signing keys or faulty message checks, not broken cryptography. A bridge holds users’ deposits in one vault and releases assets on another chain after an approval; when attackers defeat that approval path, they can take funds backed by the vault as a whole.

RenBridge is a useful example of why bridge risk includes the route back: its wrapped-asset redemption path makes RenBridge versus WBTC a practical question about whether the wrapper can still be redeemed. A bridge can move tokens across chains, but users also need a working way to exchange wrapped tokens back for the original asset.

How a few keys or a bad message drained bridge vaults

Ronin shows how a small signing group can become a single point of failure. In March 2022, attackers controlled five of the network’s nine validator keys, enough to approve withdrawals. The FBI attributed the theft to North Korea’s Lazarus Group.

Harmony Horizon had a similar weakness in June 2022: attackers compromised a two-of-five multisig. In both cases, the problem was that a few keys met the threshold needed to release assets. A larger group of signers can make that harder, but the protection still depends on how many keys an attacker must control and how those keys are secured.

Wormhole failed at a different step. In February 2022, a signature-verification flaw on Solana let an attacker submit a message that the bridge accepted as authorization. The attacker minted about 120,000 wETH without putting up collateral. The bridge’s approval check, rather than the underlying cryptography, let unbacked tokens into circulation.

Nomad’s August 2022 exploit also turned on message verification. A faulty initialization made unproven messages valid. Once people saw that the bridge accepted withdrawals, copycats repeated the exploit and drained funds. The shared lesson from Wormhole and Nomad is that a bridge must check that each cross-chain message is authentic before it releases or creates assets.

What a drained vault means for token holders

When a bridge vault is drained, the wrapped tokens on the other chain lose their backing. A holder may still see the token in a wallet, but the bridge no longer has the corresponding assets needed to redeem every token. That gap can push the wrapped token below the price of the original asset, because buyers are pricing in the risk that redemption will fail.

Measure your exposure against the whole vault, not just your own deposit. If one contract bug or a small set of stolen keys can release everything, every wrapped token that depends on that vault is exposed at once. It is like a shared cash drawer: your contribution may be small, but a single open drawer affects everyone relying on it.

Bridge risk also includes operator control. Multichain users lost access to funds when control of its keys failed. RenBridge was not hacked; it shut down after its owner, Alameda, collapsed, ending redemption. That distinction matters to holders: a token can lose its route back to the original asset through an exploit or because the people operating the bridge can no longer provide that service.

Chainalysis identified cross-chain bridges as the leading source of stolen crypto in 2022. The incidents show why: one approval path can control a vault’s full balance, so losses can match the funds held there.

Related

What happens to wrapped tokens after a bridge hack?

Wrapped tokens lose the backing that should make them redeemable for the original asset. If the vault cannot cover redemptions, the wrapped token can trade below the asset it represents.

Was RenBridge ever hacked?

RenBridge was not hacked. It shut down after Alameda’s collapse, which ended redemption and left users facing an operator failure rather than an exploit.

Bridging can make sense for an amount you expect to use and redeem soon, especially when the bridge has a large signer set or proof-based verification. Holding wrapped tokens for longer keeps you exposed to the bridge’s vault, approval checks and operators for longer. Before bridging, check how withdrawals are authorized and whether you can redeem the wrapped token when you need to.
