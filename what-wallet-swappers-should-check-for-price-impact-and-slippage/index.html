What Wallet Swappers Should Check for Price Impact and Slippage

Price impact is built into a swap quote; slippage is the change before execution. Check both, plus minimum received, route, fees and network.

The Crypto Front Page DeskOct 17, 2026 · 05:33 UTC5 min read

Price impact is the effect a swap has on the quoted exchange rate, while slippage is the difference between the quote and what the transaction delivers when it executes. Before confirming, check the estimated output, the minimum you can receive, the route and the fees. Those figures describe separate parts of the trade, and reading them together helps show what you are agreeing to.

A wallet requests a quote for the amount you want to trade. A swap service or protocol calculates a route through one or more liquidity pools, estimates the output, and may set a minimum output that the transaction must meet. The quote is a snapshot: it can change before the transaction is included onchain. For a cross-chain case, the fuller thor swap guide walks through five checks before assets move. Here, the key distinction is between the trade’s effect on the quote and later changes to execution.

What does price impact mean in a crypto swap?

Price impact is the change in a pool’s exchange rate caused by the size of your trade relative to available liquidity. In a pool-based swap, the pool’s token balances help determine the price. Taking a large amount of one token out and adding another shifts those balances, so successive units of the trade exchange at less favorable rates. A small trade in a deep pool generally moves the rate less than the same trade in a shallow pool.

Think of a pool like a see-saw: a small push moves it a little, while a larger push changes its position more. The analogy describes the direction of the effect, though actual pool formulas and routes vary. An aggregator may split a trade across pools or choose a longer route to improve the quoted output, but extra steps can add fees and network costs.

Price impact is usually estimated before you submit. It is part of the quote, not a setting that tells the transaction how much worse you will accept. If the displayed impact seems high, try a smaller amount and compare the new quote. A lower quoted impact does not automatically mean a better overall trade: account for fees and the final amount received.

What is slippage, and how is it different?

Slippage is the difference between the quoted result and the result available when the swap executes. While a transaction waits, pool prices can move because of other trades, or the available route can change. The execution price may therefore be better or worse than the quote. Price impact concerns how your trade changes the pool price; slippage concerns changes between quote and execution.

Wallets often express slippage tolerance through a minimum received amount. If the output falls below that floor by execution time, the transaction may fail instead of completing at a worse price. The exact protection depends on the protocol and wallet. A wider tolerance allows a larger difference from the quote, but it can also permit a less favorable execution. Setting it very narrowly may cause a transaction to fail when prices move, and a failed onchain transaction can still use network fees.

What should you check before confirming a swap?

Read the quote as a set of connected figures rather than relying on one percentage. Check:

  • Estimated output: the amount the route expects you to receive, after any displayed deductions.
  • Price impact: how much your trade is expected to move the pool price or worsen the rate.
  • Minimum received: the output floor tied to the slippage setting, if the wallet shows one.
  • Route and total costs: which assets and pools the swap uses, plus protocol fees and network costs.

Then compare the minimum received with the estimated output. A large gap means you are allowing more room for the quote to worsen before execution is blocked. Check that the selected network and output asset are the ones you intend to use, especially when the route crosses chains: each step can affect timing, costs and the final delivery.

When should you change the slippage setting?

Change it only when you understand why the wallet’s current setting may not fit the trade. If execution regularly fails because the output misses the minimum, a slightly wider tolerance may help, but review the revised floor before signing. If the quote shows high price impact, increasing tolerance does not fix the underlying trade size or liquidity; it only allows execution at a result farther from the quote. Reducing the amount or choosing a route with better quoted output may address that problem, though the fees and route still matter.

The practical check is simple: know what the swap is expected to deliver, how far execution may vary, and what the route costs. Price impact describes the quote’s built-in tradeoff; slippage tolerance sets a boundary for later movement. Before confirming, inspect both figures and watch whether the wallet updates the quote while you review it.
