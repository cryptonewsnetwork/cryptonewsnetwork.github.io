OCC leaves stablecoin rewards open to case-by-case review

The OCC’s GENIUS Act rule bars issuers from paying stablecoin interest directly, while leaving some third-party rewards open to case-by-case review.

The Crypto Front Page DeskOct 22, 2026 · 23:17 UTC3 min read

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) finalized its GENIUS Act stablecoin rule on October 21, leaving cryptocurrency platforms able to offer some interest-like rewards while regulators assess each arrangement. The distinction is between the company that issues a stablecoin and a platform that holds or trades it: issuers cannot pay holders interest directly, but third-party rewards are not automatically prohibited, The Associated Press reported.

How can a stablecoin reward reach a customer?

A payment stablecoin is designed to hold a steady value, usually against the US dollar. Its issuer backs the tokens with reserve assets, such as bank deposits or US Treasuries. Those assets can earn interest, but the GENIUS Act bars the issuer from paying that interest directly to people holding the stablecoin.

A separate company, such as an exchange or other intermediary, may offer a reward to customers who hold or use the token. The OCC says it will assess these arrangements case by case, looking at whether a payment is solely tied to holding, using or retaining a stablecoin, or is made at least partly for another reason. The details of the arrangement therefore matter: the rule does not give every third-party reward a blanket approval.

That issuer-intermediary split is the hinge in the policy. The OCC’s final rule sets requirements for stablecoin issuers under its jurisdiction, while its treatment of third-party payments leaves room for arrangements regulators consider distinct from direct interest. Banking Dive’s report on the rule says the OCC also distinguishes some transaction-based payments from interest or yield.

What does the rule cover, and when does it begin?

The rule implements the GENIUS Act for payment stablecoin issuance and related activities by entities subject to OCC authority. It covers requirements for reserves, redemption, risk management, audits, reporting and supervision. The OCC says the final rule and the act take effect on January 18, 2027. Separate rulemaking will address Bank Secrecy Act, anti-money-laundering and sanctions requirements.

The reward question matters because stablecoin reserves can earn returns that platforms may seek to share with customers. AP reported that Coinbase offered yields of up to 3.5% on USDC balances, compared with a 0.38% average yield for traditional savings accounts, citing FDIC figures. Banking groups have argued that higher-yield stablecoin products could pull deposits from banks and reduce their capacity to lend. Those are concerns raised by the groups, not outcomes established by the OCC rule.

What should platforms and customers watch next?

The rule does not set one universal formula for third-party rewards. Platforms will need to account for how each payment is structured and whether it is tied only to holding, using or retaining a stablecoin. The OCC says it will monitor the industry and may adjust its framework as it develops.

What changed is that the OCC finalized its stablecoin framework without categorically prohibiting third-party rewards. The next markers are the rule’s January 2027 effective date and how the OCC applies its case-by-case standard to particular reward programs.
