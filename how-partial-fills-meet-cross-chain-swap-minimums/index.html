How Partial Fills Meet Cross-Chain Swap Minimums

Partial fills let a swap execute in pieces, while cross-chain minimums set the least acceptable destination output; learn how to compare both before signing.

The Crypto Front Page DeskOct 16, 2026 · 06:04 UTC4 min read

A partial fill lets a swap execute in pieces, while a cross-chain minimum sets the least output the trader will accept on the destination chain. The order records the tokens, amount, price conditions and expiry; a solver or liquidity provider then attempts to satisfy it, sometimes in more than one transaction. The key is to check whether the minimum applies to each piece or to the completed trade.

For a regular trader, that distinction changes what an incomplete order means. A fill may deliver some of the requested tokens and leave the rest pending, or the trade may have to complete as a whole. Cross-chain swaps add another step: the source-chain deposit and destination-chain delivery may be handled by different transactions. For background on wallet-side routing, see QuickSwap route selection from your wallet. The route helps determine which liquidity and settlement path the swap uses.

How does a partial fill work?

A partial fill works by settling less than the full input amount against the order’s terms. First, the trader signs or submits an order specifying the input token, output token, amount and conditions such as a minimum rate or expiry. A solver finds liquidity and proposes a fill. The contract checks that fill against the order, transfers the relevant tokens and records how much remains.

Think of the order as a tab that can be paid down in installments: each accepted fill reduces the unpaid amount, while the original conditions govern what is left. This is useful when one pool or counterparty cannot handle the whole trade at once. Several fills can draw on different sources of liquidity, but the execution price and costs can differ from one piece to the next. Some orders allow partial execution; others require a full fill. The interface or order details should say which applies.

A trader should also check how a partial fill affects the remainder. It may stay available until expiry, be cancelled, or follow another protocol-specific rule. A fill does not by itself guarantee that the rest will execute at the same time or rate. If the order expires with an unfilled balance, the way that balance is returned or made available again depends on the swap design.

What does a cross-chain minimum protect?

A cross-chain minimum sets a floor for the output on the destination chain. The trader specifies an input on one network and an acceptable amount of another token on a second network. The route quotes the expected output after its applicable exchange rates and fees, then the settlement process must meet the stated condition or follow the protocol’s failure and recovery rules.

The minimum is a boundary, not a promise of the quoted amount. Market prices can move while a swap is being filled or settled, and bridge, network or swap costs can affect what arrives. A tighter minimum rejects more outcomes below the desired amount, but it can also make execution less likely. A looser minimum accepts a wider range of outcomes. The useful comparison is between the quoted destination amount and the minimum after all listed costs—not just the source-chain amount sent.

Cross-chain designs differ in where they enforce that floor. Some check the destination output as part of settlement; others expose separate steps or recovery conditions. Do not assume that a partial source-chain fill means the same fraction of destination tokens has already arrived. Read the order status and the protocol’s stated handling for incomplete or expired swaps.

What should traders check before signing?

Before signing, identify the amount that may be filled, the minimum output, the expiry and what happens to any unfilled balance. Then compare the route’s estimated destination receipt with the floor you are agreeing to. If the screen shows only a total estimate, look for the order terms that govern individual fills and any later settlement.

  • Fill rule: Confirm whether the order permits partial fills and whether more than one fill can occur.
  • Minimum: Check which token and chain the minimum refers to, and whether it applies per fill or to the overall order.
  • Remainder: Find out whether an unfilled amount stays open, expires or is returned under the route’s rules.
  • Costs and timing: Review listed fees and the stated settlement window, since each fill or chain step can affect the final receipt.

Partial fills make a trade easier to execute in pieces, but they can leave an order incomplete. Cross-chain minimums give the trader a defined floor for destination output, subject to the protocol’s settlement rules. What changes after signing is the order’s remaining balance and status; what to watch next is whether each fill satisfies the stated terms and whether the final destination receipt clears the minimum.
