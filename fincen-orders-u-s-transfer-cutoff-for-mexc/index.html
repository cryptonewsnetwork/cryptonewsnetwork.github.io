FinCEN orders U.S. transfer cutoff for MEXC

FinCEN ordered U.S. financial firms to block transfers involving MEXC after alleging $16.7 billion in illicit flows; the order takes effect after publication.

The Crypto Front Page DeskNov 8, 2026 · 03:52 UTC3 min read

The U.S. Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) has ordered U.S. financial institutions to stop transferring funds to or from crypto exchange MEXC Global, citing alleged illicit flows of at least $16 billion. Treasury’s Nov. 6 announcement says the activity was connected to sanctions evasion involving Iran, North Korea and Russia, as well as cybercrime. The measure targets the financial routes that connect MEXC to U.S. institutions; it does not itself shut down the exchange.

What transfers does the order prohibit?

FinCEN’s order under Section 9714 prohibits covered U.S. financial institutions from transmitting funds involving MEXC Global, including its non-U.S. subsidiaries, branches, offices and successor entities. In practical terms, U.S. institutions act as a bridge: the order tells them not to carry funds to or from the exchange. FinCEN says the action takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register. The order had been submitted for publication and was pending review when FinCEN issued it.

The authority comes from the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act. FinCEN’s formal finding is that MEXC is a foreign financial institution of primary money laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance. Treasury says the order also addresses flows linked to Iran, North Korea and cybercrime. The order does not replace existing Bank Secrecy Act reporting duties or sanctions administered by Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control.

How did FinCEN reach its $16.7 billion figure?

FinCEN says that between May 2021 and May 2026, MEXC facilitated $16.7 billion in funds connected to Russian illicit finance and other illicit activity, including sanctions evasion and cybercrime. That is the agency’s estimate of transactions it assessed as connected to illicit activity, not a claim that all the funds were proceeds of crime.

Within that total, FinCEN identified nearly $1.5 billion in direct and indirect exposure to transactions linked to Russian sanctions evasion, including $232 million in direct transactions with U.S.-, European Union- or U.K.-sanctioned entities. The agency says MEXC continued transacting with sanctioned exchanges Garantex and Grinex as recently as June 2026. It also identified about $1 billion in direct transactions with more than 40 Iranian crypto exchangers and approximately $22 million in transactions for Iran’s sanctioned central bank and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and its Qods Force.

What happens next for MEXC and its users?

The immediate next step is publication in the Federal Register; the 30-day clock begins then. Once the order takes effect, covered U.S. institutions face civil or criminal penalties for violations, Treasury says. The restriction applies to those institutions’ transfers involving MEXC; the order does not announce a global ban on trading through the exchange.

What changed is that FinCEN has issued a formal order to block U.S. financial institutions from processing transfers involving MEXC. The next date to watch is its Federal Register publication, which starts the countdown to the restriction taking effect.
