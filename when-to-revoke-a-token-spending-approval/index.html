When to Revoke a Token Spending Approval

A token approval lets a contract move specified tokens later; revoke it when the job is done, the spender is unfamiliar, or its allowance exceeds what you need.

The Crypto Front Page DeskOct 4, 2026 · 19:21 UTC4 min read

Revoke a token spending approval when you have finished using the app, no longer trust the spender, or find that the allowance is broader than you need. An approval is an on-chain instruction attached to your wallet address and a particular token: it lets a specified contract request transfers of that token later. The contract does not need you to approve every transfer separately, so an unused approval can remain active after you leave the app.

For a common ERC-20 token, the token contract records an allowance for each owner-and-spender pair. When you approve a swap, the spender is usually a contract that performs the swap, and the allowance sets how much of that token it may move. For wallet and token address checks around a swap, the fermi swap walkthrough covers the surrounding steps in more detail. This approval is separate from connecting your wallet to a website: disconnecting the site does not clear an on-chain allowance.

What can a token spending approval do?

A token spending approval lets its named spender call the token contract to transfer tokens from your address, up to the remaining allowance. The approval does not itself move tokens, and it does not give the spender control of your wallet or other token types. But if the spender contract is compromised or behaves unexpectedly, an active allowance can expose tokens of that type up to its limit.

Some apps ask for a specific amount; others ask for the maximum possible amount, often shown as “unlimited.” A large allowance can save a transaction the next time you use the app, but it leaves more authority in place than a one-time transaction needs. Think of it like giving a service a standing tab instead of paying for one purchase: convenient, but worth closing when the service is no longer needed.

When should I revoke an allowance?

Revoke an allowance when its original purpose has ended or the permission no longer matches what you intend to do. Common reasons include finishing a one-off swap, abandoning an app, seeing a spender address you do not recognize, or finding an unlimited allowance where a smaller amount would have worked.

  • After a one-time interaction: Clear the allowance if you do not expect to use that app again soon.
  • When the spender is unfamiliar: Check the token and spender addresses on the correct network before taking action.
  • When the allowance is larger than needed: Reduce it to zero or to a smaller limit, if the wallet or approval tool supports that choice.
  • When a contract changes or is reported compromised: Treat old approvals as separate permissions that may need review, even if the app still looks familiar.

For a regularly used app, keeping a limited allowance can be a reasonable trade-off: fewer approval transactions, but a smaller amount exposed if something goes wrong. For most people, the better default is to grant only the amount needed and revoke broad approvals that have no current use. The right interval depends on how often you use the app and how much convenience you want from a standing approval.

How do I revoke an approval safely?

Use a wallet’s approval view or a reputable approval checker for the relevant network, then select the token and spender whose allowance you want to change. Confirm that both addresses and the network match the permission you intend to revoke; similar token names can refer to different contracts. A revoke action generally sends an on-chain transaction setting the allowance to zero, so it may require a network fee and does not take effect until the transaction is confirmed.

Afterward, check the allowance again on that same network. If it reads zero, the spender can no longer use that allowance to make new transfers from your wallet. Revoking does not reverse transfers already made, prevent you from approving the app again later, or cancel every kind of signed permission. In particular, some token standards use different approval mechanisms, so check the permission type shown by your wallet rather than assuming every approval works like an ERC-20 allowance.

The practical change is straightforward: an approval keeps token-moving authority active beyond the transaction that first needed it. Review permissions after one-off use, after a change in trust, and whenever an allowance is broader than necessary; next, watch for which token, spender, network, and limit each new approval names.
