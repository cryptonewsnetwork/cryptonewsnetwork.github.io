Fake Crypto Bridge Websites: The Approval Check

Signs of a fake crypto bridge website include copied domains, recovery-phrase requests and approvals to attackers; inspect the spender before signing.

The Crypto Front Page DeskNov 26, 2026 · 00:42 UTC3 min read

Fake crypto bridge websites reveal themselves through look-alike domains, recovery-phrase requests and approval prompts sent to unknown spenders. They copy a real interface, then use the wallet approval to give an attacker control over assets.

A bridge moves supported assets between blockchains: you choose a route and amount, then connect a wallet and approve the transaction or token access it needs. The Polygon bridge moves supported assets between Ethereum and Polygon PoS, making its approval flow a useful point of comparison.

Five warning signs

1. The domain is misspelled or only looks familiar

A fake bridge often copies a real site’s name, logo and layout but changes its web address. Read the full domain before connecting your wallet; a swapped letter, extra word or unusual ending can send you to a copy. For the Polygon bridge, the official interface is portal.polygon.technology. Save the address as a bookmark and open it directly next time.

2. You arrived through an ad, DM or reply

A paid search result or social link can lead to a convincing clone, even when the text looks relevant. A message may also claim there is a problem with your transfer and urge you to use its link. Close that page and open your saved bookmark instead. This takes the link’s destination out of the decision: you start from the address you already checked.

3. The site asks for your recovery phrase

A bridge should not need your wallet’s recovery phrase to connect or request a transaction. That phrase can restore the wallet, so entering it gives the recipient a route to take control. MetaMask connects through wallet prompts; a webpage asking you to type the phrase into a form is a stop sign. Do not paste it into a chat, support form or “verification” box either.

4. The approval names an unknown spender

A token approval gives a named spender permission to move that token from your wallet. Treat it like handing someone a key: the spender’s identity matters as much as the site’s appearance. On an ERC-20 deposit through the Polygon bridge, the spender is the ERC20PredicateProxy contract. Compare the approval prompt with that name, then look up the contract on Etherscan before signing. If the prompt names an unknown contract or a personal address, reject it and leave the site.

Read the spender field in the wallet prompt itself; a familiar bridge logo does not identify the recipient of the permission. A request that appears before you enter an amount is another warning: the site is asking for access before you have set up the transfer. Pause and verify the route and prompt from the bookmarked interface.

5. The permission is broad or the page creates urgency

An unlimited token allowance can let a spender use more than the amount needed for one transfer. Review the amount and spender before approving; where the wallet offers it, limit access to what the transfer requires. A setApprovalForAll request grants an operator broad control over a collection of NFTs, so treat an unexpected one as a serious warning. A bridge transfer should not need you to rush past either prompt.

Bonus, airdrop and countdown claims add pressure without identifying who receives wallet access. A fake page may say the offer expires or that funds are stranded, then steer you toward an approval. Stop, close the tab and reopen the bookmarked address. Sign only after the domain, transfer details and spender all match what you intended.
