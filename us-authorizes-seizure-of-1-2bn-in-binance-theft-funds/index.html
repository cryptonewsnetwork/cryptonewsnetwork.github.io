US authorizes seizure of $1.2bn in Binance theft funds

The Justice Department secured authority to seize or restrain $1.2 billion of Binance theft proceeds, part of a wider freeze that now exceeds $2.7 billion.

The Crypto Front Page DeskNov 14, 2026 · 16:24 UTC3 min read

Investigators trace stolen crypto, service providers freeze funds they can reach, and courts authorize seizure or restraint: the Justice Department says that process has now covered more than $1.2 billion from the Binance theft. The department announced the court authorization on Nov. 12; it is part of more than $2.7 billion frozen so far through combined government and private-sector efforts, according to its announcement on actions against North Korean crypto theft.

What did the court authorize in the Binance case?

The Justice Department says that on or about Oct. 29, North Korean military members known to the FBI as TraderTraitor stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance. Since then, federal agencies have worked with virtual asset service providers, or VASPs, to trace and interdict the funds.

The distinction between a freeze and a seizure matters. A freeze can stop assets from moving while investigators work; court authorization allows the government to seize or restrain specified assets through legal process. The department says it has obtained that authorization for more than $1.2 billion of the amount already frozen. The Associated Press reports that the FBI described the action as a seizure, while noting that forfeiture proceedings can determine who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds.

Who is involved in freezing the stolen assets?

The Justice Department names its National Security Cyber Section, the Criminal Division’s Computer Crime and Intellectual Property Section, the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia and law-enforcement partners as participants. The FBI’s Los Angeles Field Office, Cyber Division and Virtual Asset Unit are coordinating recovery work with the U.S. Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation.

VASPs are part of the response because they can block transactions involving assets they handle. Last week, the FBI, Secret Service and IRS issued a cyber alert attributing the theft to TraderTraitor and urging providers to block transactions involving listed addresses, including transactions derived from them. That is like putting a stop on funds at points where they pass through a service provider; it does not by itself recover every asset.

What happens to the remaining funds?

The Justice Department says efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the crypto are continuing. It also says TraderTraitor actors are laundering assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. Those routes can move crypto across networks or services, complicating efforts to follow and block it.

The current figures describe different stages: more than $2.7 billion frozen by government and private-sector efforts, and more than $1.2 billion covered by court authorization to seize or restrain. The Associated Press report says forfeiture claims may go before a judge, who would determine legitimate claims to recovered funds. The next markers are how much more can be frozen and traced, and how the courts resolve the assets subject to forfeiture.
