Chainflip Halts Swaps After Vault Outflows; Key Exposure Found

Chainflip disabled deposits, withdrawals, swaps and quotes after vault outflows on Nov. 21; the team says it has identified how a signing key was exposed.

The Crypto Front Page DeskNov 22, 2026 · 08:47 UTC3 min read

Chainflip put its protocol into full safe mode on Nov. 21 after assets left its vaults, disabling deposits, withdrawals, swaps and price quotes while it investigated a potential exploit. The protocol says it has since identified how a key was exposed and prevented similar attacks, but has not publicly detailed the mechanism.

In a message from Chainflip Labs, the team said 122.86137042 BTC, 520.2243399 ETH, 3,249,631.41 USDC and 758,141.33 USDT were taken from Chainflip vaults that day. Separately, The Defiant reported that an Ethereum transaction moved about 520.2 ETH, 3.25 million USDC and 758,141 USDT to one recipient. The publication said Chainflip had not confirmed the cause or its estimate of the loss.

How could vault outflows affect swaps across chains?

Chainflip routes swaps between separate blockchains. Its vaults hold assets used for those trades, while its validator network authorizes transfers from them. The Defiant’s account of Chainflip’s security documentation says validators halt core functions when the protocol enters full safe mode. Think of the mode as a circuit breaker: it interrupts activity while the team investigates whether the system can safely keep moving assets.

That interruption applies beyond the chain where an outflow is reported. A user asking Chainflip to trade assets across networks depends on the protocol’s routing and vaults to complete the transfer. With deposits, withdrawals, swaps and quotes disabled, users cannot start a new trade or complete ordinary vault operations through the protocol during the halt.

What is known about the key exposure?

The initial evidence does not establish a root cause. The Defiant reported the Ethereum outflow and noted Chainflip had not confirmed either the monitor’s estimate or a proposed cause. The Block reported that security firms believed the transaction used a valid signature from Chainflip’s aggregate signing key, which they said suggested compromise of the signing system. That is their assessment, not a confirmed explanation from Chainflip.

Chainflip’s later message says the team traced preparation for the attack, identified the mechanism of key exposure and prevented future attacks. It does not explain how the key was exposed. The same message asks the recipient to return 90% of the assets by Nov. 23, keeping 10% as a bounty; that is an offer, not confirmation that funds have been recovered.

What changes next?

The immediate change is that Chainflip’s core services are paused while the team investigates and works on recovery. Its message outlines a return offer and says law enforcement could use information the team identified, but it gives no timetable for restoring service. The Defiant reported that Chainflip had also provided no restoration timetable in its initial announcement.

The next developments to watch are a public technical explanation, any confirmed recovery of the assets and an announcement about when the protocol will leave safe mode. Until then, the reported signing-key theory remains unconfirmed by Chainflip.
